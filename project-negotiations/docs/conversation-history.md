# THE NEGOTIATION TABLE

## Product concept and conversation history

**Date:** 23 November 2026\
**Purpose:** Consolidated record of the product direction discussed in
this conversation.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 1. Core concept

**The Negotiation Table** is a strategy, negotiation and
human-intelligence simulation designed to make players better
negotiators, skeptics, strategic communicators and decision-makers.

The player receives a case, studies the situation from both sides,
prepares a strategy, and enters a realistic negotiation room. They
interact with AI-controlled participants through a natural group-chat
interface, choosing whom to address and what to say.

The experience should feel like sitting across from real people and
trying to get something done---not selecting dialogue options in a
conventional visual novel.

The game should teach through experience, consequences and detailed
debriefing, rather than simply presenting negotiation theory as lessons.

## Core promise

Help players become better at: - Negotiating and reaching agreements. -
Questioning unsupported claims and detecting inconsistencies. -
Recognising bluffs and manipulation. - Bluffing or withholding
information strategically when appropriate, while understanding the
risks to credibility. - Reading incentives, personalities and group
dynamics. - Making concessions without giving away too much. - Managing
pressure, uncertainty and conflict. - Knowing when to compromise and
when to walk away.

The game should reward understanding and sound reasoning, not merely
winning.

# 2. Core gameplay loop

## Phase 1 --- The Assignment

The player is presented with a negotiation case. They may choose from
available cases or eventually receive an assigned case through a career
mode.

Each case should have: - A clear setting and context. - The player's
role. - The stakes and deadline, if any. - The parties involved. - The
player's objectives. - A meaningful possibility of success, failure,
compromise or deadlock.

## Phase 2 --- The Briefing Room

The player receives a briefing covering both sides.

**Player's side:** - Role and responsibilities. - Objectives and
priorities. - Constraints and non-negotiables. - Resources and
leverage. - Internal disagreements, if relevant. - Private information
available to the player.

**Opposing side:** - Their stated position and demands. - Their apparent
objectives and constraints. - Known resources and leverage. - Available
intelligence about their situation. - Relevant participants and
relationships.

Information must be classified: - **Verified:** established facts. -
**Reported:** claims from a source that may or may not be accurate. -
**Unknown:** information the player needs to investigate or discover.

Briefings must not reveal everything. Participants may have private
information, mistaken assumptions, incomplete knowledge or hidden
incentives. The player should not automatically know the opponent's true
position.

## Phase 3 --- Preparation

Before entering the room, the player can prepare: 1. Objectives and
priorities. 2. Minimum acceptable outcome / reservation point. 3. BATNA
--- best alternative to a negotiated agreement. 4. Available leverage.
5. Initial strategy and opening position. 6. Potential concessions and
trade-offs. 7. Questions to ask and assumptions to test. 8. Predictions
about the other side's interests and likely behaviour.

An optional strategy notebook can record assumptions and predictions.
The debrief can later compare them with what was actually true.

## Phase 4 --- The Negotiation Table

The central experience is a realistic-looking, static meeting room with
participant portraits, names and roles. It does not need a moving or 3D
room.

The main interface: - Static visual room / participant portraits. - A
group-chat-style conversation. - A way to select one participant or
address everyone. - Natural text input. - Clear indication of who is
speaking. - Optional participant dossiers in a later iteration.

The player speaks naturally rather than choosing from fixed dialogue
options.

AI participants have distinct: - Objectives and incentives. -
Personality and communication style. - Knowledge, beliefs and secrets. -
Constraints and red lines. - Relationships with other participants. -
Attitudes toward the player.

Participants should react selectively and naturally. Not everyone should
speak after every player message.

Core interaction possibilities: - Address an individual or the whole
group. - Make offers and counteroffers. - Ask questions and challenge
claims. - Persuade, compromise or refuse. - Withhold information. - Use
strategic ambiguity or bluff. - Detect and challenge potential bluffs. -
Remain silent. - Introduce new information. - Handle interruptions and
disagreements. - Reach an agreement, deadlock or walk away. - Respond to
deadlines and time pressure.

The negotiation should be open-ended and conversational, not a
multiple-choice dialogue tree.

## Phase 5 --- Debrief

The debrief is a major part of the learning experience.

It should cover: - Final outcome and whether objectives were met. -
Agreement terms and commitments. - Concessions made by each side. -
Unresolved issues and risks. - Relationship, trust and credibility
changes. - Important moments in the conversation. - What the player did
well. - What the player missed or mishandled. - Claims that were false,
incomplete or misleading. - What the other participants actually wanted,
knew or feared, when the scenario supports revealing this. - Alternative
approaches and their likely consequences. - Skills demonstrated and
skills to improve.

The player must not be judged only by whether they won. A player can
lose while reasoning well, or win through luck while making poor
decisions. The assessment should distinguish outcome quality from
decision quality.

The long-term game can support modern and historical / medieval
settings.

Potential categories:

-   Acquisitions and mergers.
-   Salary and employment negotiations.
-   Investor disputes.
-   Partnerships and contracts.
-   Labour disputes.
-   Hostile takeovers.

## Diplomacy and politics

-   Peace treaties.
-   Border disputes.
-   Coalition formation.
-   Trade agreements.
-   Hostage diplomacy.
-   Political alliances.

## Historical and medieval

-   Royal succession.
-   Territorial disputes.
-   Kingdom alliances.
-   Ransom negotiations.
-   Rebellions.
-   Resource-sharing.

## Legal and conflict resolution

-   Settlement agreements.
-   Mediation.

-   Hostage situations.

The initial release should focus on modern cases. Historical and

# 4. Skills the game should develop

## Negotiation

-   Anchoring and counter-anchoring.
-   BATNA and reservation prices.
-   Conditional offers.
-   Strategic concessions.
-   Mutually beneficial agreements.
-   Recognising when to walk away.

## Skepticism and critical thinking

-   Challenging unsupported claims.
-   Distinguishing facts from assumptions.
-   Detecting inconsistencies.
-   Asking questions that reveal information.
-   Recognising framing and logical fallacies.
-   Avoiding confirmation bias.

## Bluffing and strategic communication

Teach the distinction between: - Concealment: not disclosing one's
actual limit. - Strategic ambiguity: communicating without revealing the
full position. - Credible signalling: demonstrating that alternatives
exist. - Bluffing: making a claim or implying a position that may not be
true. - Counter-bluffing: recognising and responding to another party's
attempt.

The game should teach the risks of deception. A caught bluff can damage
credibility and future negotiations. Bluffing should not be treated as
universally good or encouraged without context.

## Psychology and people-reading

-   Understanding incentives.
-   Interpreting emotional reactions cautiously.
-   Recognising power dynamics.
-   Building trust.
-   Handling aggression.
-   Reading group dynamics.

## Strategic thinking

-   Planning multiple moves ahead.
-   Managing uncertainty.
-   Identifying leverage.
-   Making decisions under pressure.
-   Balancing short-term gains and long-term relationships.

Skill evaluation should be evidence-based and explain which player
actions support each assessment. Avoid presenting arbitrary AI-generated
scores as objective psychological measurements.

# 5. Additional gameplay features

These are valuable ideas, but not all belong in the first release.

## Hidden-truth reveal

After a case, reveal what participants actually knew, wanted or feared,
where the scenario supports it. The player may discover that: - The
apparent decision-maker was not the true decision-maker. - An opponent
was bluffing about alternatives. - A seemingly minor participant had
decisive influence. - The player's briefing contained an incorrect
assumption.

## Replay and alternative approaches

Allow the player to revisit a pivotal moment and try a different
approach: - Address another participant. - Reject an initial offer. -
Remain silent. - Ask a different question. - Make a conditional offer.

Compare the consequences of the alternative approach. Do not claim
certainty about hypothetical outcomes when they are simulated.

## Pressure mode

Some cases can include: - Approaching deadlines. - Aggressive
opponents. - Secret cooperation between participants. - Incomplete
information. - Internal divisions on the player's side. - Opponents with
substantially more leverage.

## Adaptive adversaries

Over time, opponents may notice repeated player habits. For example, if
the player always concedes after two rejections, an experienced opponent
may exploit that tendency. This should be introduced after the core
simulation is reliable.

## Knowledge library

Unlock concise explanations of concepts encountered in cases: - BATNA. -
ZOPA. - Anchoring. - Principal-agent problems. - Prisoner's dilemma. -
Information asymmetry. - Signalling theory. - Game theory. - Cognitive
biases.

Teach concepts in context rather than turning the game into a textbook.

# 6. Progression and future modes

A future career progression could include: 1. **Novice:** everyday,
personal and basic commercial negotiations. 2. **Practitioner:** complex
contracts, disputes and multiple parties. 3. **Strategist:** high-stakes
negotiations, incomplete information and political dynamics. 4. **Master
Negotiator:** multi-party diplomacy, international crises and
complicated alliances.

Progress should depend on demonstrated skills, not merely the number of
cases completed.

Potential future specialisations: - Corporate negotiator. - Diplomat. -
Crisis mediator. - Political strategist. - Legal mediator. - Independent
dealmaker.

Potential future modes: - **Campaign:** connected cases, recurring
characters and reputation. - **Challenges:** short focused exercises,
such as detecting a bluff or escaping a deadlock. - **Free
Negotiation:** choose a scenario, role and participants without campaign
restrictions.

These are future directions, not MVP requirements.

### Three proposed initial cases

**Case 01 --- The Acquisition**

The player represents a struggling company. A larger corporation wants
to acquire it. The player must secure a good deal without sacrificing
everything they have built.

Skills: anchoring, leverage, information management and bluff detection.

**Case 02 --- The Fragile Peace**

Two neighbouring states are on the verge of conflict. The player
represents one side in a multi-party negotiation. Allies have their own
interests.

Skills: diplomacy, coalition building, conflicting incentives and
compromise.

**Case 03 --- The Walkout**

Employees threaten a strike. Management claims it cannot afford their
demands. Both sides have something to lose, but neither is revealing
everything.

Skills: skepticism, emotional intelligence, questioning and creative
agreements.

# 8. Technical and simulation principles

A critical architecture rule:

**AI handles conversation and character simulation; deterministic game
logic handles authoritative state and consequences.**

## AI simulation layer

-   Generates character dialogue.
-   Maintains distinct personalities.
-   Interprets player intentions.
-   Reacts to persuasion and pressure.
-   Simulates private knowledge and interpersonal dynamics.
-   Proposes possible actions and responses.

## Deterministic game engine

-   Tracks offers and counteroffers.
-   Validates agreement terms.
-   Tracks concessions and commitments.
-   Maintains trust, credibility and relationships.
-   Checks objectives and constraints.
-   Determines outcomes and consequences.
-   Produces structured data for the debrief.

The AI may propose that a participant accepts an offer, but the game
engine must validate compatibility with that participant's constraints
and ensure all required parties have agreed.

Avoid dialogue/state contradictions. The transcript and structured game
state must remain consistent.

Use one shared LLM infrastructure with separate context for each
character, rather than requiring a separate model for every participant.
Generate only meaningful character reactions to control cost and avoid
unnecessary model calls.

Keep scenario facts, participant objectives, private knowledge,
constraints, and possible outcomes in structured data wherever
practical. Avoid relying on the model to remember all scenario rules
from a long prompt.

# 9. Product principles and boundaries

1.  The player should feel they are negotiating with people, not
    navigating a dialogue tree.
2.  The briefing must present both sides, but not reveal every hidden
    fact.
3.  The player should be able to speak naturally and address specific
    people or the whole group.
4.  Participants should have independent interests and believable
    reactions.
5.  Not every negotiation must end in agreement.
6.  Winning is not the only measure of skill.
7.  Debriefing and learning are core gameplay, not optional decoration.
8.  Bluffing should be treated as a strategic choice with consequences,
    not as a universally desirable behaviour.
9.  Modern cases are sufficient for the first release; medieval and
    other settings can come later.
10. Build one excellent end-to-end negotiation experience before
    expanding the feature set.
11. Avoid unnecessary 3D, animation, multiplayer, complex campaigns or
    expensive infrastructure in the MVP.
12. Prefer reliable, testable mechanics and low-cost AI orchestration
    over impressive but fragile complexity.

# 10. Conversation history and decisions

This section records the progression of the idea in this conversation.

## Initial concept

The user identified **The Negotiation Table** as a favourite concept,
with the ambition of creating something genuinely original and a
preference for strategy / management experiences.

The user wanted: - All kinds of roles to be possible. - Both modern and
medieval worlds eventually. - Negotiation, debriefing and related
strategic aspects.

## Interface clarification

The user clarified that negotiations should work like a group chat, but
with a realistic room and faces: - The room does not need to move. - The
player selects the person they want to talk to. - The player speaks to
that person. - AI handles the conversation and negotiations continue
naturally.

## Initial MVP direction

The user said features could be added later and wanted to focus on the
current app.

The initial MVP recommendation was: - One well-designed negotiation
scenario. - A static room with participant portraits, names and roles. -
Group-chat negotiation. - The ability to address one participant or
everyone. - AI participants with distinct interests, personalities and
positions. - A debrief showing agreement, concessions, unresolved issues
and consequences.

Features such as private meetings, complex alliances, long-term
reputation, campaigns, political simulations and intelligence gathering
were deferred.

## Learning objective clarification

The user then clarified the deeper purpose:

**The app should make people better negotiators, skeptics and bluffers
when needed.**

They also specified that before every negotiation, the game should: -
Give the player a case to take on. - Provide information and a
briefing. - Explain both sides: the player's side and the opposing
side. - Then move into preparation and negotiation.

## Expanded plan developed in response

The concept was expanded into: 1. Assignment and case selection. 2.
Two-sided briefing. 3. Preparation room. 4. Group-chat negotiation in a
realistic static room. 5. Detailed debrief and skill development.

Additional proposals included: - Modern and historical/medieval case
categories. - Verified, reported and unknown information
classifications. - BATNA, reservation point, leverage and strategy
preparation. - Natural-language offers, counteroffers, questions,
concessions and bluffing. - Selective participant reactions. -
Hidden-truth reveals. - Replaying pivotal moments. - Pressure
scenarios. - Adaptive opponents. - Contextual learning library. - Career
progression and future game modes. - A separation between AI character
simulation and deterministic game-state logic.

The agreed recommendation remains to start with a focused modern MVP
containing three strong cases, 4--5 participants per case, a reliable
negotiation engine and a high-quality debrief. Everything else should be
added only after the core loop works well.

# 11. Product identity in one paragraph

The Negotiation Table is an AI-powered negotiation and strategy
simulation in which players take on realistic cases, study both sides of
a dispute, prepare their strategy and negotiate naturally with
AI-controlled participants in a realistic group-chat meeting room. Each
participant has independent interests, knowledge, constraints and
personality. Players learn to question claims, recognise incentives,
manage information, bluff strategically when appropriate, make
concessions and reach agreements under uncertainty. After each
negotiation, a detailed debrief reveals what happened, evaluates the
player's reasoning and teaches practical lessons. The goal is not merely
to win negotiations inside a game, but to become more capable of
handling negotiations in real life.
